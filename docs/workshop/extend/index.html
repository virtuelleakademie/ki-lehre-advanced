Extend: From Tool to Practice

Duration: 15 minutes

Purpose

You now have a configured tool, a portable prompt, and a calibration record. This block is for stepping back and asking what you actually have, where it serves your teaching, and where it does not.

This is reflection and design thinking, not coding.

Question 1: tool versus recipe

Think (3 minutes)

You leave this workshop with two artifacts of different kinds:

  • The deployed tool: the shell URL with your prompt baked in. Anyone with the link can run diagnostics through your prompt against student responses.
  • The portable recipe: the exported markdown block. Pastes into any LLM you have institutional access to, including Microsoft Copilot.

When does each one serve you better?

Situation Tool Recipe Why
Quick diagnostic on a single student response in office hours
Routine triage of a class set of short answers
Sharing the diagnostic approach with a colleague
Working without a network connection
Working with sensitive student data that cannot leave the institution

Discuss (4 minutes)

Compare with your neighbour. Where you marked different cells, was the difference about your discipline, your institutional context, or your personal workflow?

The answer “the recipe is always better than the tool” is a perfectly defensible position. So is the opposite. The interesting work is in articulating why one is better for you.

Question 2: the load signal that matters most

Think (3 minutes)

Of the five load_signal categories, which one do you most need to be able to detect reliably in your discipline?

Reasons it might matter most:

  • It is the most common failure mode among your students
  • It is the one you are most likely to misdiagnose without help
  • It is the one whose intervention your teaching currently lacks
  • It is the one that distinguishes “still learning” from “needs a different approach”

Pick the category and write one sentence on why.

Share (5 minutes)

Volunteers share their pick and reasoning. The instructor will note which categories cluster across the room. The clustering itself is informative: it shows where this audience’s teaching most needs diagnostic help.

What you take back

The minimum useful thing

If you do nothing else after this workshop, you have:

  1. A diagnostic vocabulary: five categories that name what is happening in a student response, grounded in cognitive load theory and schema-acquisition research.
  2. A portable prompt: a markdown recipe you can paste into Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, or Claude on Monday morning, and use on real student work.
  3. A calibration record: knowledge of which signals your prompt distinguishes reliably and which it does not, so you know where to trust the tool and where to second-guess it.

The vocabulary is the point. The tool is the medium.
